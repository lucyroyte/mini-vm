      Welcome to Brooklyn Vision

      Reimagine Brooklyn's landscape and see what it would do for the climate. The map starts as Brooklyn is today, built from NYC Open Data.

      1. Paint the map with forests, wetlands, green streets, buildings and more.
      2. Adopt policies and pick the storm and sea level rise to plan for.
      3. Watch the report card score your vision on flooding, biodiversity, heat and carbon against today.

      Nothing you paint changes real data. Visions are saved in your browser and can be exported to share.

      About Brooklyn Vision

      Brooklyn Vision is a sketching tool for the borough's future landscape. You repaint parts of Brooklyn, and simple climate models built on public data show how the change would affect flooding, habitat, heat and carbon, and who lives where it matters. It is inspired by visionmaker.nyc.

      Ways to use it

      Urban planning

      Test a rezoning, a new park or a waterfront plan at the scale of blocks and neighborhoods. Compare options side by side by saving each as a vision, and check how much green space it takes to cool a heat island or keep a street from flooding.

      Policy change

      Turn on borough-wide rules like the Unified Stormwater Rule or low-flow toilets and see what they would do for sewer overflows and street flooding. Pair a policy with a planting plan to show what neither achieves alone.

      Community engagement

      Bring the map to a community board, block association or public workshop. People can paint what they want their neighborhood to become, see the trade-offs right away, and export the result to share or submit.

      Education

      Teach how cities and climate connect: why paved ground floods, why trees cool streets, how sea level rise reaches the shore. Students can set a goal, such as raising the heat score by five points, and work out how to reach it.

      Advocacy and storytelling

      Make the case for a greenway, a restored marsh or a depaved schoolyard with numbers and a map, and show how many residents live in the places that would benefit.

      Research and early screening

      Find where a change would matter most before spending on detailed study. Every cell carries its data sources, so results can be traced back and checked.

      What it can and can't tell you

      The models are quick screening estimates meant for comparing ideas, not engineering studies. Flooding uses a simple rain budget and the city's Stormwater Flood Maps rather than a full sewer model, and some land is mislabeled in the source data, such as Green-Wood Cemetery showing as park lawn. Treat the score as a guide to which ideas are worth a closer look.

      Data comes from NYC Open Data, the US Census, USGS Landsat and terrain data, NYC Planning and FloodNet. The full list is under Borough → Data sources in the side panel.
